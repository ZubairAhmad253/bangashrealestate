When you decide to build on your plot, one of the first things a builder will ask is: **grey structure or turnkey?** The two terms are used everywhere in Rawalpindi and Islamabad, but they are not always defined the same way by every contractor. Understanding exactly what each includes will help you compare quotes properly, avoid disputes later and choose the option that suits your time, budget and involvement.

## What grey structure means

Grey structure (sometimes called the "structure" or "shell") is the skeleton of the house: everything that holds it up and divides the space, before any finishing. It usually includes:

- Excavation, foundation and plinth, with damp-proof course (DPC)
- RCC columns, beams and roof slabs
- Brick masonry for external and internal walls
- Staircase and the roof-top room (mumty)
- Electrical conduits and plumbing or drainage pipes laid inside walls and slabs
- Underground water tank and septic or sewer line, depending on the contract

What is often **not** included: plaster (some contractors include it, some don't), flooring, doors and windows, woodwork, kitchen, bathroom fittings, wiring and switches, and paint.

Grey structure contracts are commonly offered in two forms: **with material**, where the contractor supplies cement, steel, bricks, sand and crush along with labour, and **labour only**, where you buy the materials and the contractor provides the workforce. Labour-only can look cheaper, but you take on the work of buying materials, checking quality and managing deliveries.

## What turnkey means

A turnkey (or "complete") contract covers the grey structure **plus all the finishing** needed to hand you a house that is ready to live in. Along with the structure, it typically includes:

- Plaster, putty and paint
- Tiles or marble for floors, stairs and bathrooms
- Doors, windows, grills and railings
- Kitchen cabinets and woodwork
- Electrical wiring, switches, distribution board and light points
- Sanitary fittings, geysers and water connections
- Boundary wall, main gate and porch finishing

Even "turnkey" varies. Items like air conditioners, light fixtures, wardrobes, false ceilings, solar systems and landscaping may or may not be part of the package. A good turnkey agreement lists them clearly.

## Side-by-side comparison

| | Grey structure | Turnkey |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Structure and shell only | Structure plus finishing, up to handover |
| Your involvement | High during finishing; you hire and manage each trade | Lower; one contractor manages everything |
| Control over finishing | Full control over every purchase and its timing | Choices agreed in the specification at the start |
| Accountability | Split between several contractors | One party responsible for time and quality |
| Spending | Easier to pause or spread over time | Paid in agreed stages through the whole project |
| Main risk | Coordination gaps and blame between trades | Disputes if the specification is vague |

## When grey structure makes sense

Grey structure suits you if:

- You want to build in phases and arrange the finishing budget later
- You enjoy selecting every tile and fitting yourself, and have the time to visit markets
- You already have reliable electricians, plumbers and carpenters you trust
- A family member can be on site regularly to supervise the finishing

The downside is coordination. When the plumber, tile fixer and electrician are separate, problems such as a leaking pipe under a new floor can turn into an argument about who pays for the repair.

## When turnkey makes sense

Turnkey is usually the better fit if:

- You live abroad or in another city, as many overseas Pakistanis building in Rawalpindi and Islamabad do
- You have a demanding job and cannot visit the site often
- You want one party to be responsible for the timeline, quality and final result
- You prefer agreeing the full specification upfront to reduce surprises

The key to a good turnkey project is a **detailed specification**. "Good quality tiles" is not a specification; a named brand and range, or a clear per-unit allowance, is.

## What to put in writing either way

Whichever option you choose, your agreement should include:

1. **Scope of work** and a bill of quantities (BOQ)
2. **Material specifications:** steel grade, cement brand, brick class and concrete mix for the structure; brands, ranges or allowances for finishing
3. **Payment schedule** tied to completed stages, not dates
4. **Who handles approvals:** society map approval and any other permissions
5. **Site supervision** and how you will receive progress updates
6. **Changes:** how design changes during construction are priced and approved
7. **Handover:** final inspection, snag list and how defects found after handover will be fixed

## Don't save money on the structure

Finishing can be upgraded later; the structure cannot. The foundation, steel and concrete decide how safe and durable your house is. Make sure the work follows the approved map and the structural drawings, check steel before each casting, and insist that slabs and columns are cured properly. Cutting corners here to afford better tiles is a poor trade.
